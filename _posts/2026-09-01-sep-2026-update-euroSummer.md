# Life during September 2026 and euro maxxing


## Current life and the trip

![Euro summer](/images/euro-summer.JPEG)
{: style="max-width: 700px; width: 100%; display: block; margin: 20px auto;"}

I have my final exams coming up so naturally, I thought it would be the perfect time to write something. So I will write. Finally some interesting stuff (euro summer) happened to me. You can skip to the [survival and pro tips guide](/blog/2026/09/02/lifehacks.html) if you want to skip reading my existential dread and self loathing monologue of despair and agony. 

I somehow managed to pass through 5 years of engineering. I am on the verge of graduating unless I manage to fail analog circuit lab or my research supervisor forgets to grade me. Despite acquiring insomnia, terrible posture, and deep seated trust issues along the way, my single greatest achievement remains keeping a full head of hair. My strategy was always to sit at the front, listen to the lecture with focus, and then study right before the exam. This is very efficient and has given me good grades (B+ in a GED course) but sadly does not work for mathematics or similar courses, which is 90% of engineering, where understanding the material is not enough and you actually have to practice it. The advantage of this strategy is that you save a lot of time by not studying, which you can spend better by walking around listening to Radiohead and thinking you are better than everyone else. And you have more time for reading actual books, like The Count of Monte Cristo, and not Calculus by the Sullivan brothers.

Enough about education. Recently I had the opportunity to travel to Europe for an academic course on Artificial Intelligence. As I will officially hit unc status this December, I saw it as more than a study tour. You are in your 20s and time moves too fast. You open your phone and see that the friend you saw two weeks back is already on her second marriage, and you cannot decide what song to put on Spotify while showering (always go with Beach House in this scenario). I grew up watching friends and family travel the world and heard their stories. I heard them so much it felt like I had almost physically been there. I would occasionally fantasize about travelling to the west. I was lucky enough to go to India and Thailand as a kid, but never far beyond the centre of the equator.

So when I finally got the Schengen visa I decided that this would be the trip that gave me peace, equanimity, or whatever the Greeks called Ataraxia and the Sadhus call Moksha.

There were 5 of us from our university chosen for the summer school in Normandy. Two other boys from my own department, and two girls from pharmacy and biochemistry. I learned from Thailand in 2022 that your travelling companions make or break your trip, so I was hesitant to go with them and figured I would travel solo. This would give my soul the chance to lie in the sun, explore nature, drink espresso, read Urdu poetry and surf the Kali Yuga. Also it would save me money. So I booked my flight for three days after theirs.

I did not want to go to a new country, take a bunch of pictures, post them on story (nothing is wrong with that), and stay inside my own little world. I did not want the same gentrified areas everybody goes to, the same copy-paste café, the same order. I went with the mindset that travel is never a perpetual highlight reel, that there is a lot of mundane boredom and grueling effort in between.

I was wrong about a lot of this, but it took me a while to notice.

## The journey

The transit flight from Dhaka to the Middle East is always the hardest part. I was the last one to board because apparently they could not verify my visa. A two week summer school did not make sense to Etihad Airways as a reason to give me a boarding pass. I showed them my VFS details in my email and finally got on the plane.

I got the middle seat, which is always the worst to have. You don't get the window view and you lack the mobility to get up. There was a constant ASMR of babies crying. The guy on my right was on his phone the whole time letting everyone know he was in an aeroplane. "Bhai ami flight e uthsi." "Bhai ami plane e asi." He was from Sylhet and had mastered the art of subtle flexing. I wish I had asked him what his sim operator was, because he was still talking when the plane was flying. The guy on my left was reading Plane e uthar dua. So I knew I was safe. He told me to be aware of pickpockets in Paris. He was a real one for that. He was from Chittagong and was going to Portugal from Abu Dhabi. Most people on my flight were either going to Italy or Portugal.

Abu Dhabi is by far one of the best airports. The staff are friendly and will actually help you. I got a shawarma and sat on a bench near my gate to charge my phone. There were two boys speaking French. Since I was going to France, I asked them the easiest way to get to Saint-Lazare from the airport, because I had a train from Paris to Normandy the same night I landed.

One of them spoke English very well. His name was Adrien. He had just graduated in sports science and wants to help athletes reach their full potential. It was World Cup season so naturally we talked about football, since France were doing well and had a chance to win. He said he would love to see France win but he doesn't care much about football and is more of a rugby fan. He had recently gone through a breakup and was on his own arc of finding himself. I said that's fine, I went through something similar recently too.

Then he gave me a full itinerary. What to do and what not to do in France, the places I had to see, the places I could skip, the best ice cream in Paris (Berthillon). I wrote the whole thing down.

There is a certain intimacy in sharing all manner of personal details with a stranger in an airport whom you will never meet again. It set the rhythm of the trip perfectly.

The flight from Abu Dhabi to Paris was calmer and there were no babies crying. I got the middle seat again, this time between two French girls. I asked the one on my right if she wanted to swap, because I can't sit still for long and I like being able to get to the toilet without disturbing anyone, but she said she was comfortable in the middle. She was French Algerian and, funnily, was coming back from Indonesia, the same place Adrien was going. She read the dua for being on a flight, the second person to do it in front of me in two days. She also guessed my age correctly, which was surprising because I look young for my age. The girl on my left had wired iPhone headphones in and was larping.

## Arrival

Charles de Gaulle did not meet my expectations. I was excited to be in Paris and my first hour there was not great. The airport is big and not hard to get around but the staff were nonchalant and had a look of apathy on their faces. I had forgotten that you go through immigration police before baggage claim, so I was looking for a luggage belt that did not exist yet and could not find it and was dumbfounded. I was in a rush and panicking a bit. The jet lag and the continuous journey had got to me.

I had a ziplock bag with me. In it were my return tickets, my accommodation details, the dorm information, the university letter. I had been assembling that bag for weeks. It was the bag of a person who has been told his entire life that the burden of proof is on him.

The officer was an old man in glasses. He was relaxed and he spoke slowly. He asked for my hotel reservation, I showed him the summer school documents, and he was satisfied and did not ask me anything else. I was stuffing everything back into the ziplock as fast as I could so the person behind me could go next, and he said:

> "Calm down, Monsieur. Don't rush."

I said merci. Every time I panic I still remember him saying it.

After that I was dead tired. Taking the train would mean changing stations twice with my luggage and a lot of stairs, so I decided on a taxi. When the taxi people asked if I needed one I instantly said yes, and before I could fix a fare one of the drivers had already picked up my bags and put them in his car. I asked him the fare. He said 56 euros. I said no and took my bags back out, and then realised I couldn't escape that place anymore.

The next driver was called Wali. It was a fixed cost from CDG into Paris anyway. I asked if he could show me some landmarks on the way to the station, because I would regret landing in Paris and not seeing the Eiffel Tower. He said he could, for 20 euros more. I thought about it and said fine.

I saw the Arc de Triomphe, the Louvre, the Tuileries, and the Eiffel Tower from up close. Wali taught me the days of the week. Dimanche. Merci. S'il vous plaît. He put on French music for me, a song called Parisienne. He showed me pictures of his wife and daughter. He made sure I saved his number on WhatsApp before I got out.

It cost 80 in the end, because he didn't have change.

That is the most expensive taxi ride of my life (almost 11 thousand 400 taka). I had to be more frugal for the rest of the trip because of it. At least I got to see the Louvre and listen to Farooque Bhai Project.

## Normandy

My time in Normandy was planned out by the program. The classes were long and covered everything from robotics to pharmacy to business marketing. The lecturers were well prepped, but I was jet lagged and groggy most of the time. We still clutched and ended up one of the best groups in the hackathon.

I hate networking. I live in a constant fear of being asked to share a fun fact about myself, and would much rather connect with someone serendipitously.

At one point we were all craving deshi food. It was the idea of our mutual Bangladeshi friend, who had come from Saskatchewan Canada, and who turned out to be an excellent chef. The dorm kitchen was small — one stove, one sink, an oven, and five people who all wanted to be useful. The two girls could not cook. We made khichuri with dim and murgi. Bangladeshi home cooked food always hits different, and it hits differently again in Normandy. I was a bit sad that evening because Egypt had lost to Argentina by unfairness.

I had booked my flight three days late specifically to avoid these people. But they were great. 

## Back to Paris

Afterwards I went back to Paris.

The first night in the hostel I met my roommate, a Mexican guy, and it was the first night for both of us. He was also a CSE student, already graduated, now travelling around Europe. He said he thought people from Bangladesh were super smart in the CSE department, I smiled and nodded. We were both wrecked from the journey.

Our third roommate was a drag queen. He did his makeup in the room, wore a gold jacket and knee socks, and used a perfume you could smell from the corridor. It was all too much for the first night. We went out for a walk partly so as not to be in the room.

So the two of us walked down to the Seine. There were people playing football and we joined in, one touch and afterwards we went exploring the desolate alleyways off the river until it got late. Manuel gave me a Mexican coin and I gave him a Bangladeshi one.

I had been lonely and bored for a good part of that day. Getting into Paris is always troublesome. Then I met two people and it was fine. That is roughly the entire emotional weather system of travelling alone, and it turns over about twice a day.

I went to Shakespeare and Company because of the Lost Generation, and because of Before Sunset, and because I wanted to buy a book with a Shakespeare and co stamp on it. It happened to be a weekend, and Patti Smith was at the café next to the shop doing a book signing. Its the second best book shop I have ever been to. Obviously the first is book worm Bangladesh in Shahabuddin Park. I hope I get to see that store again soon.

Then I went to Rome and Florence and finally Dhaka.

My last night in Paris was the 13th of July. The Bastille Day fireworks are usually on the 14th, but that night they put on the show over the Eiffel Tower, and at one point the drones formed three Eiffel Towers in the sky above the real one.

So my final image of Paris was two counterfeit Eiffel Towers standing next to the original, which I had first seen from the back seat of Wali's taxi, on day one, for twenty four euros extra.

## What changed

So what changed. What did I learn and gain. Was I able to successfully immerse myself.

Well, I got Eurotanned and I know how to claim a VAT refund in Europe.

Honestly, I keep thinking about what [Tony Soprano](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/zxA1wAjCWJU?feature=share) said, that there is no geographical solution to an emotional problem. It is easy to believe a trip will fix your inner turmoil. But saying that leaving the familiar changes nothing at all would be just as reductive, and it isn't true either.

Yes, the Eiffel Tower is nice for ten minutes. But a heatwave makes you appreciate the real taste of water, which somehow feels more special than a historic landmark standing in front of you. When you walk forty thousand steps and spend the day avoiding pickpockets, you end up happy with very simple things.

Kahneman called it System 1, the fast automatic thing your brain does when nobody asks it to. Most of my life runs on it. Simone Weil spent a lot of her short life on the other thing, and the part I keep returning to is that she did not think it was effort. Attention, for her, was not concentrating harder. It was subtraction. You put down what you arrived carrying, and only then can anything reach you. She called it the rarest and purest form of generosity.

![Simone Weil](/images/simone-weil.webp)
{: style="max-width: 700px; width: 100%; display: block; margin: 20px auto;"}

> Rather than the contracting of our muscles, attention involves the canceling of our desires; by turning toward another, we turn away from our blinding and bulimic self. The suspension of our thought, Weil declares, leaves us "detached, empty, and ready to be penetrated by the object." To attend means not to seek, but to wait; not to concentrate, but instead to dilate our minds. We do not gain insights, Weil claims, by going in search of them, but instead by waiting for them.

It is easy to feel alienated and lonely in one of the loveliest places on earth. But if you put some of it down and pay attention, you notice that people have been generous with you the entire time and you were too busy to see it.

Your mother packing your bag heavy with things you didn't ask for. Your travel buddy carrying your gondho cheese in his bag. The old French lady helping you order a baguette with no jambon so you can eat halal. The Mexican roommate who gives you a Mexican coin, and the two of you going for a walk because you're both scared of the third one. Your friends back home keeping you updated on all the academic stuff so you don't have to worry about it. The Bangladeshi migrants in Italy who go out of their way to make sure you are fed and looked after. An old man in glasses at a border telling you to calm down.

As cliche as it sounds, maybe the real journey was the friends we made along the way.

## Closing thoughts

If you have read this far, thank you for your patience. For you I wish - less.

Less information, less food, less entertainment, less communication, less stimulation. You already have too much of all that, and it stands in the way of your serenity, health, sleep, and creativity.

Make sure to treat your body like a data center by drinking plenty of water and feeding your mind with literature. Au Revior.
