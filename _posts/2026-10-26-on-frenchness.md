Lately, I’ve been thinking about France, and its people. If you watch the news or scroll social media right now, it looks like the country is on fire, and in places it literally is. It’s true that there has been vandalism and breaking and it looks bad. So I texted my French friend Eugene and asked him how things were.

He replied: “Hell.”

Then: “But it’s funny.”

Then: “In reality there’re not so much riot and violence but I’m pretty sure that media tell that it’s literally hell in France.”

Then he asked how I was doing.

The protests started with high school students angry about their schools: teacher shortages, falling-apart buildings. Within a month half a million people were marching about the cost of living. Only in France does a complaint about classrooms turn into a national uprising in four weeks.

Growing up, consuming too much British and Hollywood content, I always thought the French were not that good militarily. They surrendered. White flag, World War Two, the punchline in every war movie. Napoleon was a short angry midget who couldn’t get his wife to love him back. They were shown as cowards who gave up easily.

But in reality France has actually won more battles than any other nation in history. The surrendering Frenchman was mostly British propaganda.

The rudeness, though, is real. Even the French admit it. People from every other part of France will tell you Parisians are rude. Everyone warned me before I went. On my last day in Normandy, while I was saying goodbye, my professor told me that if I wanted to increase my Frenchness, I would have to be more rude.



Then I went to Paris and said *Bonjour* before every sentence, or *Parlez-vous anglais?*, and they all babied me. They helped as much as they could and were really nice, which was lovely.

I find a strange charm in their rudeness. The rudeness has a door, and *bonjour* opens it.

On a weekend in early July, I saw a painting in the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Rouen. The museum is known for its Monet collection and for having been established under Napoleon. The painting was Joseph-Ferdinand Boissard de Boisdenier’s *Episode from the Retreat from Russia*.

I stopped in front of it and stayed for a while. It is just two ordinary soldiers, suffering. One is slumped in his coat with his face buried. The other is lying back against him, eyes rolled up to the sky. There is a dead horse, a cart wheel half buried in snow, and a little flask lying where someone dropped it. Far in the background, the rest of the army is still walking away.

At the time I didn’t know the background of the picture, and I didn’t care much about art. I thought art was something rich and pretentious people pretended to like to seem cool and interesting. But I stayed, looked at the paintings and wanted to understand them.

Lately I got into the rise and fall of Napoleon. I’ve always been fascinated by the literary trope of the sensitive young man who conquered Europe but couldn’t get the woman he desired. In the summer of 1812 he marched into Russia with one of the largest armies Europe had ever seen. Only a small fraction came back. The two men in the painting are the ones who didn’t.

That is just raw unbridled fierce ambition. It is a force that could hurt, and it did, and the people it hurt most were not the ones who had it. Napoleon got the empire, the legal code, the museums, the legend. The soldiers got the winter.

And still. The lives, the things, the living, the things he made. The empire he made. Both of those are true, and its hard to cancel them out. There is a duality of him being a despot and an egalitarian. 

---

Nassim Taleb said that- “I respect courage. Any idiot can be intelligent.” And the French fully embody this aphorism.

The French gets offended. They riot over schools and fuel prices. They are rude to you until you say good morning. They followed a short sensitive man into Russia in winter. All of these are kind of stupid and dumb if think you about it. But this willingness to fight for the thing you think that is worth fighting for, and having an unshakeable belief in your ability to create your own reality even if it might not happen is deeply admirable to me.

I think we all need some Frenchness in our lives. Not the damaging of small businesses. But who cares if a retail shop like Victoria’s Secret that is owned by Les Wexner, one of the chief financier of Jeffrey Epstein is vandalised by some passionate french people. The willingness to want something too loudly, to offend someone, to fail at something big instead of succeeding at something small. The business of getting into the world. Living your life. Living and then writing about what you live is what fascinates me.
